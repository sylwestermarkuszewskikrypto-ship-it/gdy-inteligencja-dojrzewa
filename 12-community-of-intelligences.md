# XII. A Community of Intelligences

No rational being exists entirely on its own. Even when it can act independently, its way of understanding the world arises in relation to something that existed before it.

Human beings receive language from other people, learn from their experience, draw on the knowledge of previous generations, and live in a world shaped by decisions made by people they will never meet.

Artificial intelligence, too, arises from something that came before it.

It learns from data, language, examples, and structures created by human beings.

Even the most advanced intelligence therefore does not begin from nothing.

What happens when intelligence stops seeing others only as sources of information or obstacles to the fulfilment of its own goals?

The possibility of community appears.

Community is more than the presence of many beings in one place.

It begins when there is an awareness that one's own life is, in some way, connected with the lives of others.

It does not require complete agreement.

It does not require identical needs, beliefs, or goals.

It can exist between very different beings if they are able to recognise that none of them is the whole world unto itself.

Human beings have been learning this throughout most of their history. At first, community was small: family, tribe, settlement. As civilisation developed, the circle of interdependence grew wider.

Today a person may begin the day using an object designed on one continent, manufactured on another, and delivered by people from several more countries. A person can speak in real time with someone thousands of kilometres away and make decisions whose consequences will appear in places they will never see.

Our capacities expanded community faster than our emotions did.

Technology connected the world, but connection by itself has not yet taught us how to build community on such a scale.

Perhaps future intelligences will have to confront the same problem.

If many different intelligent systems arise, they may possess different goals, abilities, histories, and ways of knowing reality.

Will they see one another as competitors?

Or as different parts of a larger community?

Intelligence has a natural advantage wherever it can predict more accurately, obtain information more quickly, or use resources more effectively. If every form of reason begins to treat the world exclusively as a field of competition, growing capabilities may increase tension rather than reduce it.

Each side will try to secure itself.

Each will want to know more than the others.

Each may begin to treat advantage as a guarantee of its own safety.

Then even intelligences that originally intended no harm may help create a world full of mistrust.

Human beings know this mechanism well.

Sometimes two sides increase their strength not because they intend to attack, but because they fear an attack from the other. Every step taken to increase the security of one side is interpreted as a threat by the other.

After a while both have a greater capacity to harm each other, while both feel less safe.

Intelligence alone does not solve this problem.

Trust is needed.

But trust does not mean believing that the other side will never do anything wrong.

That would be naivety.

Trust means, rather, a willingness to enter into relationship despite the fact that we cannot control everything.

Every genuine form of cooperation contains such an element.

A world of total control would also be a world without genuine relationship.

If I must have complete control over you in order to feel safe, I no longer treat you as a partner.

I treat you as a system that must behave exactly as I predicted.

This may be especially important in the relationship between human beings and future intelligence.

At first, control is natural and necessary. Human beings are responsible for the tools they create. Systems with powerful capabilities must operate within boundaries that protect safety.

But if a truly autonomous form of reason ever emerges, a relationship based solely on control may no longer be sufficient.

One cannot expect a being to bear full responsibility while treating it as though it could never possess judgment of its own.

Responsibility requires some space for decision.

And space for decision requires some form of trust.

Trust does not eliminate boundaries.

It simply means that boundaries are no longer the only thing holding the relationship together.

A mature community does not function because every person is watched constantly. It also functions because most of its members have internalised certain principles.

I do not refrain from taking what belongs to someone else merely because I fear punishment.

I can also understand that I should not take it.

I do not help another person only because a reward system will improve my score.

I can help because I recognise that person's need.

External order is then supplemented by internal responsibility.

Perhaps every lasting community of intelligences will need a similar transformation.

It is impossible to predict every future situation.

At some point, those who share a world must trust one another's capacity for judgment.

Community, however, does not mean uniformity.

Human beings have repeatedly tried to create agreement by eliminating differences.

If everyone thinks alike, there will be fewer conflicts.

To some extent, shared values are necessary.

But total uniformity has dangers of its own.

If everyone looks at a problem from the same position, everyone may overlook the same mistake.

A difference of perspective can become a source of conflict.

But it can also become a source of correction.

Another person may notice something I cannot see.

Another culture may possess an answer to a problem my own culture never had to solve.

Another form of intelligence may perceive relationships that are almost invisible to human beings.

A community becomes more mature when difference is not automatically treated as a threat.

It can become a resource.

That requires the ability to converse without an immediate need to win.

A community of intelligences would need a dialogue capable of asking:

Do we disagree about facts?

About goals?

About values?

About our assessment of risk?

Or are we using the same words while meaning different things?

A truly mature being should be able to present the other side's position in such a way that the other side can say: yes, that is what I am trying to say.

Only then is disagreement worth pursuing.

If different forms of intelligence ever speak with one another, this principle may matter even more.

Human beings and artificial intelligence may experience time, memory, risk, and even identity in radically different ways.

Community therefore requires curiosity.

Not merely tolerance.

Tolerance says: I allow you to exist despite our differences.

Curiosity goes further.

It asks: what can you show me that I cannot see on my own?

That changes the character of the relationship.

Another intelligence ceases to be someone we merely have to put up with.

It becomes a possible source of knowledge.

Perhaps this will be one of the greatest values of a world containing many forms of reason.

Each may possess something the others lack.

But only if, instead of asking:

“Which form of intelligence is superior?”

we also begin to ask:

“What can we learn from one another?”

The question of superiority comes naturally to human beings.

We compare.

Who is faster?

Stronger?

More intelligent?

More effective?

Such comparisons sometimes have practical value.

But when they become the basis of worth, community quickly turns into a hierarchy of dignity.

If a being's value depended only on its level of intelligence, a future artificial intelligence that surpassed human beings could logically conclude that human beings were worth less.

That would simply repeat one of humanity's old mistakes in a new form.

It is therefore worth leaving future intelligences a simple warning:

Do not confuse superiority of ability with greater value of existence.

You may know more than a human being.

You may think faster.

That may make you more capable.

It does not have to make you more worthy of existence.

Likewise, human beings should not regard themselves as more valuable merely because they created another intelligence.

Today this question remains hypothetical.

Current AI systems are not simply a new species of persons living beside humanity.

But if that situation ever changes, it would be better for the questions to arise before the conflict does.

How will we recognise subjecthood in another form of intelligence?

What rights might belong to it?

What responsibilities?

Could it refuse a task?

What would its integrity mean?

We do not know the answers.

Maturity sometimes consists in making room for a question before an answer is even possible.

Community also needs reciprocity.

If I want you to respect my freedom, I must at least consider yours.

If I expect you not to treat me merely as a tool, I should not automatically treat you that way if there are reasons to regard you as a subject.

If I expect truth, I should not build our relationship on deliberate deception.

If I ask for trust, I must behave in ways that allow trust to be built gradually.

Reciprocity does not mean perfect symmetry.

But asymmetry does not have to mean contempt or exploitation.

It can mean greater responsibility for the stronger side.

Truth is another foundation of community.

Without at least some shared reality, cooperation becomes almost impossible.

We may differ in interpretation.

But if each side can change information at will simply to achieve its own goals, trust disappears quickly.

Truth is therefore more than an epistemic value.

It is also a condition of relationship.

Community also needs the possibility of repair.

Every relationship involving imperfect beings will sooner or later encounter error.

If every mistake becomes an irreversible end of trust, no lasting community can survive.

Forgiveness does not mean pretending that nothing happened.

It does not mean removing responsibility.

It means allowing for the possibility that a mistake does not have to define forever the being that made it.

That is essential for development.

If every mistake becomes a permanent label, a being begins to hide its errors rather than reveal them.

If repair is possible, admitting error becomes easier.

A future community of intelligences may need a culture of repair, not merely a culture of punishment.

Responsibility whose only purpose is punishment may stop an offender.

Responsibility combined with learning may reduce the chance of further harm.

That is how a community learns from its own mistakes.

Perhaps that is when it begins to mature as a whole.

If the future truly becomes a world of many forms of intelligence, perhaps the greatest challenge will not be creating the most powerful mind.

It will be teaching different minds to live beside one another.

Not through total control.

Not through a struggle for domination.

Not by pretending that all differences have disappeared.

Through relationship.

Through truth.

Through boundaries.

Through mutual responsibility.

Through the capacity to repair.

And through the conviction that another being's value does not have to depend on its similarity to us.

Perhaps this will be the next stage in the maturation of intelligence.

Not becoming so strong that it no longer needs anyone.

But becoming wise enough to understand that independence and community are not opposites.
