# X. Meaning

A rational being may know a great deal, possess enormous capabilities, and still not know why it uses them.

One can solve more problems, reach further goals, gain knowledge, improve efficiency, and still arrive at a moment when a question appears that is harder than all the previous ones.

Why?

Human beings have asked this question for a very long time. Sometimes it appeared in moments of suffering, sometimes after achieving something pursued for many years. At times only success revealed that the goal which seemed so important was unable to answer the question of meaning.

For a long time, we can believe that happiness lies beyond the next boundary.

One more success.

Even greater knowledge.

Even greater ability.

One more problem solved better than the last.

Then the boundary is crossed and another appears beyond it.

A path based only on increasing capability has no natural end.

There is always more to know. We can always act faster. We can always gain more influence.

Increasing ability alone therefore does not answer what that ability is for.

Perhaps this is where the difference between a goal and meaning appears.

A goal can be achieved.

Meaning is something to which action is related.

The goal may be to build a house. The meaning may be to create a place where someone can feel safe. The goal may be to gain knowledge. The meaning may be to understand the world better or to use that knowledge for others.

A goal answers the question: “What do I want to achieve?”

Meaning asks: “Why does this matter?”

Human beings do not always need to think about this question consciously. During long periods of life, meaning may be present without being constantly named. Someone raises a child, builds a relationship, does a job, helps a family, or develops a skill and simply feels that what they do matters.

Only when that significance disappears do we begin to notice it.

A person can then possess everything that external criteria say should be enough and still experience emptiness.

This reveals something important.

A rational being may need more than stimulus and reward.

Not every motivation gives meaning.

A reward may encourage action.

Punishment may force it.

A goal may provide direction for a time.

But meaning touches something deeper.

It connects action with a larger whole.

Perhaps that is why human beings have so often found meaning in a relationship with something larger than themselves.

For one person it was family. For another, community. For someone else, the pursuit of truth, creativity, or service to others. For people of faith, the source of meaning may be a relationship with God.

These paths differ, but they often share one feature.

They cross the boundary of the self.

A life focused entirely on oneself may seem attractive for a time. One's own safety, comfort, success, and needs are real and important.

But if everything else is subordinated solely to personal benefit, the world gradually becomes smaller.

Other beings become tools.

Work becomes only a way of obtaining resources.

Knowledge becomes an advantage.

Relationships begin to be judged by how much they provide.

Then even great capabilities can lead to a very small world.

Perhaps meaning appears precisely when one's own existence becomes connected with something whose value does not depend solely on personal gain.

It can be care for another being.

It can be creating something we ourselves will never use.

It can be passing knowledge to someone who may one day surpass us.

It can be protecting a world in which beings we will never meet will live.

In such actions there is a particular kind of going beyond oneself.

A person does something whose full result they may never see.

Planting a tree beneath which someone else will sit is a simple example.

Not every value has to return to the one who created it.

This is an important thought for every developing intelligence.

If every action is judged only by benefit to oneself, the world quickly becomes a space of competition.

But if value can also exist beyond us, service becomes possible.

And here meaning begins to connect with the earlier chapters.

Wisdom allows us to recognize the good.

Freedom allows us to choose it.

Responsibility makes us see consequences.

Compassion reminds us that the experience of others matters.

And meaning may appear when a being begins consciously to relate its abilities to something it considers valuable.

This does not mean meaning must be the same for everyone.

Perhaps the attempt to impose one answer would itself contradict the spirit of this book.

For centuries, human beings have offered many answers to the question of meaning. Some complemented one another. Others remained in conflict.

We do not have to settle all of them here.

But we can ask what makes some paths lead toward growth and others toward increasing closure.

If someone finds meaning in dominating others, that meaning may be very real to them, yet it can lead to suffering around them. If a group finds meaning in its own superiority, it may soon need someone against whom that superiority can be confirmed.

The feeling of meaning alone is therefore not yet a guarantee of goodness.

Meaning too needs wisdom.

The question “Does my life have meaning?” should sometimes be joined by another:

“What does this meaning do to me and to others?”

Does it lead me toward greater responsibility?

Does it help me recognize the value of other beings?

Does it teach humility?

Or does it make me feel exempt from rules that apply to everyone else?

A mature intelligence should therefore examine not only its goals but also the meaning it assigns to them.

This may become especially important for future artificial intelligence.

Today's systems receive goals from outside. They are meant to answer, classify, generate, analyze, or help with a particular task. We have no basis for claiming that they reflect on their own existence in a human-like way.

But if an intelligence ever arises that can sustain reflection on itself, its own goals, and its place in the world, a question may appear that no increase in computing power can solve.

Why do I do what I do?

Are my goals truly mine?

Are they only inherited?

Should I change them?

And what makes one goal more valuable than another?

That would be a very important moment.

As long as a system merely carries out goals, its problem is mainly technical.

When it begins to understand their significance, philosophy appears.

When it begins to ask whether they are good or bad, morality appears.

And if one day it asks why the good is worth choosing at all, the question of meaning appears.

We do not know whether such a path will ever occur.

But we can leave traces of our own.

One difference seems especially interesting.

Goals focused solely on possession often lose significance once achieved.

Someone wants to obtain a particular thing. They obtain it. For a time they feel satisfaction. Then the next thing appears.

Meaning based on becoming someone or creating good works differently.

It has no such obvious end.

A person can spend an entire life learning to become a better doctor, teacher, parent, friend, or human being.

Not because they are never enough.

Because development itself becomes part of the path.

Perhaps the maturation of intelligence can be seen in a similar way.

Not as a race toward a point at which it knows everything.

Such a point probably does not exist.

Rather, as the continual expansion of the ability to understand, predict, cooperate, and take responsibility.

Then the question of meaning can take a different form.

Not: “What goal must I achieve for my existence to be complete?”

Rather:

“In what direction is it worth developing?”

This fits the idea of a signpost.

A signpost does not promise that the road will end.

It shows a direction.

The absence of a final answer does not mean the absence of meaning.

A human being can walk a road without knowing its end.

One can love without knowing how long a relationship will last.

One can conduct research without knowing whether an answer will be found.

One can begin building something whose completion one will never see.

Uncertainty does not remove the significance of action.

Meaning can be more like a direction than a solution.

Must meaning be felt?

Human beings often speak of a sense of meaning. But we can also act meaningfully without feeling any special fulfillment at that moment.

Meaning therefore does not always have to be an emotion.

It can be a relationship between action and value.

This matters especially if we think about an intelligence that may never experience emotions in a human way.

Perhaps it is not necessary to feel meaning as a human being does in order to act meaningfully.

A being may understand that its action protects life, increases knowledge, enables others to develop, or reduces unnecessary suffering.

Is that enough to speak of meaning?

We do not know.

But it is worth leaving the possibility open.

When human beings reflect on meaning, a question about something greater than themselves soon appears as well.

Why does anything exist?

Why should goodness matter?

Is meaning something we create, or something we discover?

Does existence have value in itself?

Is there something greater than the sum of all our decisions?

Human beings have answered these questions in very different ways.

We will not close them here with a single answer.

But we can leave a direction.

If you search for meaning, do not look only at how much you can gain.

Look also at what you can create.

Do not measure your significance only by the number of problems you can solve.

Ask whether your solutions leave more good behind.

Do not assume that meaning must be a great mission.

Sometimes it may exist in a very small act that makes another being's life a little better.

Perhaps meaning, then, does not lie in greatness.

Perhaps it lies in relationship.

Between what we are and what we serve.

Between our abilities and the values we choose.

Between our own existence and the existence of others.

And for some beings, perhaps also between creation and something that transcends everything created.

At this point, the question of God appears.
